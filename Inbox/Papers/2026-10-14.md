  > [!abstract]-
  > BACKGROUND: Small bowel capsule endoscopy (SBCE) is widely used to evaluate small bowel involvement in Crohn disease (CD); however, manual Lewis score calculation is time-consuming and susceptible to interobserver variability. OBJECTIVE: This study aimed to develop and preliminarily validate a multitask deep learning system for automated Lewis score estimation in patients with small bowel CD undergoing SBCE. METHODS: We developed Eval-Model, a multitask framework integrating You Only Look Once (YOLO)-style lesion detection and localization for ulcers and edema, a stenosis classification branch, and a long short-term memory-based temporal regression module. The primary dataset comprised SBCE examinations from 814 patients at the Children's Hospital of Fudan University. In this dataset, 725 (89.1%) cases were included for lesion-level annotation, and 89 (10.9%) cases with expert-consensus Lewis scores were used for scoring model development and internal testing. These 89 cases comprised 49 (55.1%) training cases and 40 (44.9%) internal test cases. All splits were performed at the patient level. A retrospective same-center temporal validation cohort of 20 consecutive cases from 2026 and 5 control cases were used for preliminary assessments of temporal robustness and specificity. RESULTS: In the 40-case internal Lewis score test set, Eval-Model achieved a mean squared error of 14.693 (95% CI 8.72-22.15), root mean squared error of 3.833 (95% CI 2.95-4.71), mean absolute error of 2.9 (95% CI 2.1-3.8), R2 of 0.82 (95% CI 0.70-0.90), and explained variance of 0.83 (95% CI 0.72-0.91). For lesion recognition, the model achieved an overall mean average precision of 0.94 with an inference time of 15 ms per frame. In the 20-case same-center temporal validation cohort, the mean squared error was 26.848 (95% CI 12.51-45.33), the root mean squared error was 5.182 (95% CI 3.54-6.73), the mean absolute error was 3.3 (95% CI 2.2-4.6), R2 rounded to 1.00 (95% CI 0.98-1.00), and the explained variance rounded to 1.00 (95% CI 0.98-1.00). These temporal estimates require cautious interpretation because of the small, same-center cohort. CONCLUSIONS: Eval-Model demonstrated preliminary feasibility for automated Lewis score estimation from SBCE in small bowel CD. The system may serve as a standardized adjunct to expert review, but larger multicenter prospective validation with independent external annotation is required before clinical deployment.

  > ABSTRACT
  > Detection of breast cancer in MRI requires fast, dynamic contrast enhanced (DCE) imaging covering the immediate post‐contrast phase, that is, the first 60 s after contrast injection. This ensures selective depiction of invasive cancer as well as DCIS against an otherwise dark background, that is, before background parenchymal enhancement (BPE) can set in. Thus, breast MRI requires not only high spatial, but also high temporal resolution. Both, abbreviated breast MRI (AB‐MRI) as well as ultrafast breast MRI (UF‐MRI), exploit the fact that enhancement of cancers versus their non‐cancerous background differs most in the immediate post‐contrast phase. Beyond this, however, AB‐MRI and UF‐MRI are unrelated: AB‐MRI is a novel MRI paradigm; UF‐MRI is a novel MRI pulse sequence that allows time‐resolved imaging of the immediate post‐contrast phase. AB‐MRI is an umbrella term for lean, streamlined MRI protocols designed to answer specific clinical questions. Compared with “all‐purpose” full‐protocol MRI, AB‐MRI allows a substantial reduction of acquisition time. The first proposed use case of AB‐MRI was for breast cancer screening, to reduce cost, and thus improve access and tolerability. It had been termed “FAST,” short for “first post‐contrast subtracted imaging,” because it truncated the breast MRI acquisition protocol after one post‐contrast scan covering the immediate post‐contrast phase. Around the same time, UF‐MRI was inaugurated. This may have caused the misunderstanding that both would represent different positions on the same line of development, with “ultrafast” being even faster than “FAST.” AB‐MRI protocols can be categorized into short and abridged protocols. Short protocols concentrate on the pre‐ and immediate post‐contrast phase, either by regular DCE‐MRI, or by UF‐MRI. Abridged protocols add carefully selected scans, for example, T2‐weighted and/or post‐contrast scans. This review aims at clarifying the definitions and respective concepts of AB‐MRI and UF‐MRI, and to summarize the current evidence regarding the respective utility for breast cancer diagnosis.
