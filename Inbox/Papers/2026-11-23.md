- [ ] **Primary sclerosing cholangitis: epidemiology, risk factors, pathophysiology, clinical features, diagnosis, and management.**
- [ ] Relevant
- [ ] Read later
  Song J, Wang B, Xia Q
  Molecular biomedicine | 2026-11-21
  [10.1186/s43556-026-00662-7](https://doi.org/10.1186/s43556-026-00662-7) | Score: 0.96
  Nearest seed: Circulating multi-omic signature of disease severity and cholangiocarcinoma in primary sclerosing cholangitis.

  > [!abstract]-
  > Primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) is a rare cholangiopathy characterized by multifocal inflammation and fibrostenotic remodeling of the bile ducts, cholestasis, and increased risks of cholangiocarcinoma and colorectal cancer. No pharmacological therapy has yet improved transplant-free survival. Clinical care therefore depends on accurate phenotyping, surveillance, treatment of biliary and intestinal complications, and timely liver transplantation. Population-based and clinical studies define the epidemiology, risk factors, presentation, diagnosis, and management of PSC. Studies of human biospecimens, including microbiome and metabolomic analyses, describe biological changes associated with the disease, whereas organoid and animal models are used to investigate potential mechanisms. The available evidence links genetic susceptibility and PSC-associated inflammatory bowel disease to cholangiocyte injury, immune activation, disturbed bile acid homeostasis, and microbial alterations. At the bile duct interface, cholangiocyte senescence and barrier dysfunction may interact with microbial exposure and immune-cell recruitment to promote periductal fibrosis. PSC is therefore more plausibly viewed as a multicellular process than as the consequence of a single microbial, immune, or bile acid pathway. Whether dysbiosis and altered bile acid profiles contribute to disease development or instead reflect established PSC remains unresolved. Concomitant inflammatory bowel disease, medication use, biliary obstruction, and previous biliary interventions further complicate causal inference. Longitudinal human studies may distinguish early drivers from secondary changes. Mechanistic findings should ultimately improve patient phenotyping, surveillance, endoscopic management, and selection for targeted trials. Such trials will require biologically informed designs and endpoints that capture durable clinical benefit.

  Nearest seed: A Review of the Clinical Applications of Artificial Intelligence in Abdominal Imaging.

  > [!abstract]-
  > Abstract
  >
  > Pancreatic cystic lesions (PCLs) are prevalent and potential precursors of pancreatic cancer. Cancer risk assessment is a crucial step in PCL management, but the complexity and limited accuracy of existing approaches contribute to suboptimal patient care. We developed and validated an artificial intelligence (AI) model that uses radiology reports and clinical data to predict the future risk of pancreatic cancer in patients with PCLs. The model was developed and tested in a 13-year retrospective cohort comprising 23,398 abdominal CT/MRI examination reports from 8707 patients with PCLs across a multisite healthcare system. Of these, 430 (1.8%) corresponded to patients who later developed pancreatic cancer, defined as adenocarcinoma, invasive mucinous neoplasm, or high-grade dysplasia. Among the models evaluated, XGBoost achieved the highest performance, with AUCs of 0.960, 0.945, and 0.944 for predicting pancreatic cancer incidence at 1, 3, and 5 years after imaging, respectively. The model outperformed guideline-based risk stratification, with AUC improvements of 0.161 to 0.235 (
  > P
  > < 0.001). For examinations conducted within a year before cancer diagnosis, radiologists identified potential cancer in 51.5% of cases, while the AI model flagged 82.3%. These findings support the potential of AI to complement existing approaches for individualized pancreatic cancer risk stratification in patients with PCLs.
