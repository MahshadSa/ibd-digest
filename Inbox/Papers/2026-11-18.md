  > Abstract
  >
  > Background
  > Patients with clinically stable small-bowel Crohn’s disease (SB-CD) may harbor heterogeneous residual inflammation. We performed the first study characterizing inflammatory burden with video capsule endoscopy (VCE), intestinal ultrasound (IUS), and biomarkers, comparing their prognostic performance.
  >
  >
  > Methods
  > Pooled analysis of 3 prospective cohorts of adults with SB-CD in remission/mild symptoms (Crohn’s disease activity index [CDAI] &lt; 220) undergoing concurrent VCE, IUS, C-reactive protein (CRP), and fecal calprotectin (FCP) assessment. Primary outcome: inflammation-driven CD flare ≥6 months from baseline. Unsupervised clustering identified inflammatory clusters (n = 87); outcome analyses included 81 patients with follow-up.
  >
  >
  > Results
  > Among 87 patients (median age 31, 57% male), 3 clusters emerged: low-burden (n = 48), biomarker-predominant (n = 18; markedly elevated FCP/CRP, preserved bowel wall thickness [BWT], intermediate Lewis score [LS]), and concordant-active (n = 21; uniform elevation across modalities). Twenty flare events (25%) occurred at median 12.5 months. Concordant-active carried higher flare risk than low-burden (adjusted hazard ratio [HR] 2.67, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.01-7.11), while biomarker-predominant showed no significant excess risk (HR 1.00). In the SB-restricted comparison, SB-BWT plus FCP showed discrimination comparable to LS (C-index 0.635 vs. 0.596), and LS did not add to this combination (likelihood ratio test P = .223). Whole-bowel BWT ≥ 4.3 mm added to LS (P = .008), whereas SB-BWT ≥3.8 mm did not (P = .384).
  >
  >
  > Conclusions
  > Isolated biomarker elevation without structural activity was not associated with significant excess flare risk in clinically stable SB-CD, while concordant activity showed higher risk requiring larger-cohort confirmation. Within the SB, IUS plus FCP showed no significant difference in prognostic performance compared with VCE, with whole-bowel IUS additionally contributing colonic transmural information not captured by the LS.

  > [!abstract]-
  > Inflammatory bowel disease is a chronic immune-mediated disorder arising from interactions between genetic susceptibility, environmental exposure, microbial signals and immune regulation. Although genome-wide association studies have identified over 300 risk loci, known common variants and current genetic risk scores capture only part of disease liability and have limited ability to predict disease course or therapeutic outcomes. Environmental exposures acting across the life course are therefore likely to be substantial contributors of residual risk, yet mechanistic frameworks capable of integrating these diverse and time-varying influences remain incomplete. Together, these advances position epigenetics as an important framework for understanding inflammatory bowel disease heterogeneity and informing clinical translation.

  Nearest seed: From signals to systems: the epigenetic-microbiome-mitochondrial axis in IBD pathogenesis.

  > [!abstract]-
  > Abstract
  > Inflammatory bowel disease is a chronic inflammatory disease characterized by mucosal immunity dysregulation, epithelial barrier dysfunction, and gut microbiome dysbiosis. While cytokine-targeted therapies have improved clinical outcomes, many patients remain refractory or relapse, emphasizing the need for additional therapeutic approaches. Recent evidence suggests that mitochondrial metabolism in the intestinal epithelium regulates mucosal homeostasis, immune cell recruitment, and susceptibility to inflammation. However, it remains controversial whether mitochondrial dysfunction is a cause or a consequence of intestinal inflammation. Epithelial energetic deficiency and barrier dysfunction are associated with impaired oxidative phosphorylation, defective short-chain fatty acid utilization, redox imbalance, and faulty mitochondrial quality control. Indeed, impaired mitophagy promotes mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) release through voltage-dependent anion channel 1 (VDAC1) oligomerization and mitochondrial permeability transition pore opening. Extracellular mtDNA acts as a damage-associated molecular pattern, activating cyclic GMP-AMP synthase-stimulator of interferon genes, toll-like receptor 9, and NLR family pyrin domain-containing 3 inflammasome pathways that amplify mucosal inflammation. These metabolic changes alter luminal oxygen and microbiome composition, whereas microbial metabolites, particularly tryptophan-derived indoles and kynurenines, modulate epithelial mitochondrial function through aryl hydrocarbon receptor signaling. These findings support a bidirectional microbiome-mitochondria-immune axis that potentiates intestinal inflammation. In this review, we summarize the current evidence of how epithelial mitochondrial dysfunction associates with intestinal inflammation. We discuss how mitochondrial metabolism interfaces with microbial ecology and immune regulation to shape disease pathogenesis. We also discuss therapeutic strategies targeting this axis: mitochondrial-targeted antioxidants (eg, MitoQ), VDAC1 inhibitors, bile-acid derivatives (eg, tauroursodeoxycholic acid), and metabolite-based therapies to restore microbial tryptophan metabolism. These approaches target mitochondrial bioenergetics and microbial metabolic cross-talk with the potential to restore microbiome-immune homeostasis, preserve barrier integrity, and complement conventional immune-directed therapies.

  > [!abstract]-
  > OBJECTIVES: While clinical and endoscopic remission (ER) are established therapeutic targets in ulcerative colitis (UC), endoscopic healing may not fully reflect residual microscopic inflammation. Histologic remission (HR) has been proposed as a deeper marker of disease control and a potential predictor of long-term outcomes. We evaluated whether HR predicts relapses in pediatric UC patients who achieved ER. METHODS: This multicenter retrospective cohort study included pediatric patients with UC (<18 years) who achieved ER (Mayo endoscopic subscore (MES) 0 or 1). HR was defined as a Nancy index score of 0. Patients were stratified into HR and non-HR groups. CONCLUSIONS: HR was achieved in only one-third of pediatric patients with UC with ER but was strongly associated with reduced relapse, supporting HR as a treatment target beyond ER.
