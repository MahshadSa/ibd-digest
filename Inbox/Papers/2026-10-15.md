  Nearest seed: The Role of Imaging in Inflammatory Bowel Diseases: From Diagnosis to Individualized Therapy.

  > [!abstract]-
  > Hybrid molecular imaging is increasingly recognized as a valuable tool for the precision management of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). Driven by the treat-to-target paradigm, the noninvasive quantification of transmural inflammation and the accurate differentiation of stricture phenotypes remain key clinical challenges. Current evidence indicates that although 2-deoxy-2-¹⁸F-fluoro-D-glucose (¹⁸F-FDG) positron emission tomography (PET) can effectively quantify the overall inflammatory burden, the integration of fibroblast activation protein inhibitor (FAPI)-targeted PET imaging provides important complementary value for characterizing fibro-inflammatory remodeling and distinguishing fibrotic strictures. By combining high metabolic sensitivity with high-resolution multiparametric soft-tissue characterization, PET/magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has demonstrated potential advantages in assessing penetrating complications and facilitating longitudinal therapeutic monitoring. Pediatric and young adult patients requiring long-term follow-up may derive substantial benefit from the reduced radiation exposure offered by integrated PET/MRI systems. To overcome conventional method limitations, including physiologic intestinal uptake and attenuation-correction artifacts, the field is actively exploring novel molecularly targeted tracers, ultra-low-dose long-axial-field-of-view whole-body systems, and artificial intelligence-based image reconstruction techniques. Given that substantial current evidence is derived from small, single-center cohorts and experimental studies, these advanced modalities currently serve as adjunctive, problem-solving tools rather than routine replacements for standard endoscopy or conventional cross-sectional imaging. This review comprehensively evaluates the clinical performance, applicability, and translational barriers of hybrid molecular imaging in IBD, underscoring the urgent need to establish standardized, multicenter consensus protocols that can guide its future implementation in individualized therapeutic strategies.

  > Current treatments for inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) lack real-time inflammatory monitoring and lesion-specific drug delivery, resulting in poor efficacy and systemic toxicity.   > Protein-energy malnutrition is a common complication in Crohn's disease (CD). In addition to weight loss, patients may develop significant alterations in body composition, including skeletal muscle depletion, which can negatively affect clinical outcomes and quality of life. Herein, we report on a patient with newly diagnosed CD who presented with severe malnutrition and myopenia, which persisted despite improvements in nutritional intake and body weight. A 27-year-old woman was admitted with abdominal pain, decreased appetite, nausea, vomiting, and progressive weight loss (10 kg, 22% of her usual body weight). Based on clinical presentation, family history, and magnetic resonance enterography findings, stricturing CD was diagnosed. Nutritional assessment revealed severe malnutrition (body weight, 31 kg; body mass index, 12.26 kg/m2; phase angle, 4.4°, intracellular-to-extracellular mass ratio, 0.75). Computed tomography-based body composition analysis demonstrated marked skeletal muscle depletion (skeletal muscle index, 27.5 cm2/m2), confirming myopenia. The patient was treated with corticosteroids, infliximab, azathioprine, and multidisciplinary nutritional management. After 12 months of follow-up, she achieved clinical remission and gained approximately 25% of her initial body weight. Despite significant weight recovery and improved dietary intake, computed tomography (CT) revealed low skeletal muscle mass and increased visceral and subcutaneous adipose tissue, indicating persistent myopenia. This case highlights the importance of comprehensive body composition assessments in CD. Clinical remission and weight gain may not accurately reflect recovery of skeletal muscle mass. Early nutritional evaluation and longitudinal body composition monitoring may be helpful in the management of patients with CD to identify persistent muscle depletion and optimize long-term outcomes.

  > To review socioeconomic and racial or ethnic inequalities in CRC incidence, screening and diagnosis, treatment, surveillance and survival and assess interventions designed to reduce them.
  >
  >
  > Methods
  > We conducted a narrative review of adult studies published in English between January 2000 and March 2026. MEDLINE, EMBASE, PubMed, and ISI Web of Knowledge were searched for studies addressing socioeconomic or racial and ethnic disparities in CRC, using a saturation approach.
  >
  >
  > Results
  > Socioeconomic disadvantage and belonging to some racial or ethnic minority groups were associated with higher CRC incidence within high‐income countries, lower screening participation, later diagnosis, less timely or guideline‐concordant treatment, poorer surveillance and worse survival. In the USA, five‐year survival was 60.6% among Black patients versus 66.7% among White patients, with little improvement in this gap over several decades. Inequalities at successive stages may contribute to cumulative disadvantage. Intervention evidence was markedly uneven across the continuum. Mailed stool‐test outreach and patient navigation increased screening among underserved populations, whereas evidence for interventions specifically reducing inequalities in treatment, surveillance, and broader survivorship was sparse.
  >
  >
  > Conclusions
  > Improving overall CRC prevention and care cannot be assumed to reduce inequalities. The major evidence gap has shifted from documenting disparities towards demonstrating how to reduce them, particularly beyond screening. Equity should become an explicit outcome of CRC research, implementation, healthcare quality assessment and clinical guidelines.
