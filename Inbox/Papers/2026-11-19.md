  > [!abstract]-
  > Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a complex and recurrent gastrointestinal condition with limited therapeutic options. p-Coumaric acid, a phenolic compound widely present in fruits and vegetables, served as the starting scaffold for derivatization. Among a series of short-chain fatty acid acylated derivatives of p-coumaric acid methyl ester (p-COAMe) via the phenolic hydroxy group, pentanoylated p-COAMe (Pent-p-COAMe, PCM) emerged as the most effective compound against UC in animal and cell models. In adults, CDI has been linked to increased long-term colectomy risk, but data on its impact in paediatric IBD remain scarce. We aimed to assess the medium-term course of IBD after CDI in children. METHODS: We conducted a retrospective, single-centre, case-control study in the paediatric Gastroenterology and Nutrition Department at Necker-Enfants Malades Hospital.   Nearest seed: Handheld intestinal ultrasound and the point-of-care questions in pediatric IBD: a commentary—authors’ reply

  > [!abstract]-
  > OBJECTIVE: Monitoring pediatric inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) typically requires invasive or resource-intensive testing. Intestinal ultrasound (IUS) is a noninvasive, radiation-free, point-of-care modality increasingly used in pediatric IBD. Whether IUS exposure influences parent preferences for disease-monitoring strategies remains unknown. METHODS: We conducted a cross-sectional survey of parents of children with IBD at a tertiary center. Parents, recruited from a prospective IBD biobank with linked clinical data, were stratified by prior IUS exposure based on clinical records/imaging history. The survey assessed parent preferences, perceived discomfort, and perceived usefulness for five monitoring modalities (IUS, colonoscopy, cross-sectional imaging [magnetic resonance imaging/computed tomography], blood tests, and stool studies), as well as parent activation (parent-patient activation measure), medication adherence (parent-adapted Morisky Medication Adherence Scale), and visit impact. RESULTS: Sixty-two parents completed the survey (31 IUS-experienced, 31 IUS-naïve). Compared with IUS-naïve parents, IUS-experienced parents were more likely to report definite preference for IUS (83% vs. 36%, p = 0.003), rate IUS as completely useful (67% vs. 27%, p = 0.002), and report no perceived discomfort with IUS (90% vs. 68%, p = 0.01); conversely, they were more likely to report great discomfort with cross-sectional imaging (37% vs. 12%, p = 0.04). Trends toward greater perceived visit benefit included higher trust in physician recommendations (97% vs. 81%, p = 0.07) and greater sense of control over their child's IBD (90% vs. 71%, p = 0.08). Parent activation and adherence were high across groups. Qualitative responses highlighted reassurance, speed, and noninvasiveness of IUS. CONCLUSIONS: Parents report strong acceptance of IUS, with prior exposure associated with greater preference, perceived usefulness, and lower perceived burden.
