  > BACKGROUND: Artificial intelligence (AI)-based computer-aided detection (CADe) systems are increasingly used in colonoscopy but are trained mainly in non-inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) populations. The performance of AI-based CADe for detecting colitis-associated dysplasia compared with established imaging techniques remains unclear. OBJECTIVE: This study compared colorectal dysplasia miss rates (DMR) between a non-IBD-trained CADe system (ENDO-AID CADe, Olympus) and narrow-band imaging (NBI) in patients with IBD undergoing surveillance colonoscopy. METHODS: We conducted a unicentric, open-label, quasi-randomized crossover trial including IBD patients undergoing colorectal cancer surveillance. Each patient underwent colonoscopy with both CADe and NBI in alternating order. The primary outcome was the per-lesion DMR; a per-patient analysis was also performed. NBI detected more hyperplastic lesions (66.7% vs 28%; P < .001). CONCLUSIONS: Dysplasia miss rates did not differ significantly between CADe and NBI, although wide CIs limit definitive conclusions and findings should be considered hypothesis generating. These results support further evaluation of AI as an adjunct to established endoscopic imaging, while highlighting the need for AI models specifically trained in IBD populations.

  > [!abstract]-
  > Digital pathology (DP) is evolving from an image-based diagnostic modality into a quantitative platform for computational precision oncology. This transition is particularly relevant in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), where limited tissue specimens must support histological classification, immunohistochemistry, broad molecular profiling and an expanding range of therapy-linked biomarkers. Artificial intelligence (AI) can assist conventional tasks such as tissue segmentation, cell detection, quality control and biomarker quantification, but it can also derive image-based signatures associated with molecular alterations, spatial immune organization and treatment response. This review focuses primarily on NSCLC and examines the continuum from foundational image-analysis tasks and AI-assisted PD-L1 scoring to histology-based molecular prediction, computational companion diagnostics, pathology foundation models and multimodal AI. We discuss recent lung-specific evidence, including computational triage of actionable alterations, the emerging TROP2 quantitative continuous scoring paradigm and selected protein biomarkers associated with antibody-drug conjugates. We also consider the integration of pathology with genomics, transcriptomics, liquid biopsy, radiology and longitudinal clinical data. Emerging paradigms such as digital twins and agentic AI are discussed as research directions rather than established clinical tools. We argue that the principal value of AI in NSCLC will not be autonomous diagnosis, but the reliable conversion of fragmented data streams into transparent, auditable and clinically actionable patient representations.
